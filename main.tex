\begin{abstract}
Software engineering practice is filled with myths---beliefs derived from intuition, experience, and conventional wisdom---that lack empirical foundations.
Many of these myths are \emph{causal} in nature, concerning the consequences of decisions, interventions, and changes to practice.
While empirical software engineering research frequently investigates these myths through descriptive, correlational, and qualitative evidence, it rarely engages with the challenging question of \emph{whether} the evidence can actually support the causal claims beneath these myths.
In this thesis, I argue that \emph{causal credibility assessments are feasible in empirical software engineering research through explicit articulations of causal theory and assumptions}.

To demonstrate this argument, this thesis starts with an accessible introduction to the modern causal inference toolkit for software engineering researchers.
I will cover three conceptual pillars that would enable explicit articulations of causal theory and assumptions in empirical settings: potential outcomes framework, graphical causal models, and design-based identification.
I will walk through AI coding tool adoption as a running example throughout, demonstrating how progressively stronger causal research designs can be made through articulating a causal theory and inspecting the plausibility of assumptions beneath each statistical method used.

Then, across three empirical studies, I demonstrate how causal credibility assessments can reveal systematic gaps between practitioner beliefs and empirical reality. 
First, using counterfactual analysis and simulations on npm dependency data, I challenge the myth that pinning dependency versions improves project security, showing that it paradoxically increases attack exposure unless upstream packages pin their own dependencies. 
Second, through a longitudinal analysis of GitHub starring patterns with panel regression models, I challenge the myth that fake GitHub stars provide sustainable promotional benefits, showing instead that these artificial signals become long-term liabilities. 
Finally, using a difference-in-differences design with staggered adoption, I challenge the myth that AI coding tools unambiguously improve developer productivity, finding that Cursor's initial velocity gains are offset by sustained accumulation of technical debt.
In each study, I assess the credibility of causal claims through the following four-step framework: (1) deriving an explicit causal theory from software engineering domain knowledge, (2) defining the causal estimate of interest and specifying the assumption under which the estimate carries a causal interpretation, (3) honestly acknowledging and mitigating limitations and known caveats, and (4) thoughtfully navigating alternative explanations.
Collectively, this thesis provides a methodological blueprint for future empirical software engineering research, illustrating how to balance rigor with practicality to generate actionable, trustworthy evidence for software engineering myths.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Causal Inference for Empirical Software Engineering: A Tutorial}
\label{chp:background}

\chapterfrontmatter{%
Empirical software engineering research frequently investigates causal questions---whether code review reduces defects, whether AI-assisted development improves velocity, whether dependency pinning improves security---yet such questions are routinely answered with descriptive, correlational, or qualitative evidence and a perfunctory ``correlation does not imply causation'' caveat.
A literature review of 5{,}341 main-track papers at ICSE, FSE, and ASE between 2015 and 2025 reveals a structural gap: 29\% of empirical studies are motivated by a causal research question, but only 1.9\% employ a recognized causal inference method, and the ratio has remained stable for a decade.
This tutorial provides a self-contained primer on the modern causal inference toolkit for software engineering researchers, covering the potential outcomes framework, graphical causal models, and design-based identification strategies (difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, panel fixed effects).
A worked example on the impact of AI coding tools in open-source software illustrates the full arc from naive cross-sectional comparison through DAG-justified regression to design-based longitudinal identification, demonstrating how progressively stronger designs shrink an inflated +911\% naive estimate to a credible +32--52\% difference-in-differences estimate.
The tutorial closes with a pragmatic stance and an empirical standard for causal inquiries in software engineering research.%

\chapter{Empirical Study I: The Impact of Dependency Pinning on Supply Chain Security}
\label{chp:pinning}

\chapterfrontmatter{%
Recent high-profile supply chain attacks have led practitioners and security scorecards to advocate \emph{pinning} dependencies to specific versions as a defense against malicious package updates, while a long line of software engineering research has argued that pinning accumulates technical debt and blocks the propagation of security patches.
Despite the controversy, no quantitative evidence existed about the actual impact of pinning versus floating dependencies on the underlying security and maintenance trade-offs.
This chapter presents a counterfactual analysis on a sample of 10{,}000 npm packages and 10{,}000 GitHub repositories using npm, leveraging an experimental time-traveling feature of the npm dependency resolver to simulate the evolution of dependency graphs under pinned and floating regimes.
Using fixed-effects panel regression, we find that pinning direct dependencies not only increases the cost of maintaining vulnerable and outdated dependencies, but---counterintuitively---increases exposure to malicious package updates in larger dependency graphs because of the specifics of npm's dependency resolution mechanism.
We further show through ecosystem-wide simulations that coordinated pinning at a small set of important upstream packages, especially when paired with a hypothetical \emph{transitive pinning} feature, can reduce the spread of malicious updates by up to 75\%, providing evidence in favor of ecosystem-level interventions over local ones.%

\chapter{Empirical Study II: The Impact of Fake GitHub Stars on Repository Promotion}
\label{chp:fake-stars}

\chapterfrontmatter{%
GitHub stars are a widely used proxy for repository popularity and have been adopted as an evaluation criterion by hiring managers, investors, journalists, and academic studies---creating strong incentives to artificially inflate them.
This chapter presents the first systematic, global, and longitudinal measurement study of fake stars on GitHub.
We build \SystemName, a scalable detection tool that mines GitHub's complete event metadata for two complementary signatures of inauthentic starring activity: low-activity accounts that engage in nothing beyond starring, and lockstep clusters of accounts that star bursts of repositories together.
Applied to all GitHub events between 2019 and 2024, \SystemName identifies six million suspected fake stars across 26{,}254 repositories, with fake-star activity surging dramatically in 2024.
The accounts and repositories involved exhibit highly trivial activity patterns, and the majority of fake-star campaigns target short-lived phishing-malware repositories, with the remainder promoting AI/LLM, blockchain, and tutorial content.
A longitudinal panel regression analysis shows that fake stars provide only a brief promotional advantage---typically less than two months---before becoming a long-term liability associated with reduced organic engagement, indicating that the practice is counterproductive even for those who deploy it.
The findings carry implications for GitHub platform governance, software developers using stars as a signal, and supply chain security researchers studying malware distribution.%

\chapter{Empirical Study III: The Impact of Cursor AI on Open-Source Projects}
\label{chp:cursor}

\chapterfrontmatter{%
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated the promise to revolutionize the field of software engineering.
Among other things, LLM agents are rapidly gaining momentum in software development, with practitioners reporting a multifold increase in productivity after adoption.
Yet, empirical evidence is lacking around these claims.
In this chapter, we estimate the causal effect of adopting a widely popular LLM agent assistant, namely Cursor, on development velocity and software quality.
The estimation is enabled by a state-of-the-art difference-in-differences design comparing Cursor-adopting GitHub projects with a matched control group of similar GitHub projects that do not use Cursor.
We find that the adoption of Cursor leads to a statistically significant, large, but transient increase in project-level development velocity, along with a substantial and persistent increase in static analysis warnings and code complexity.
Further panel generalized-method-of-moments estimation reveals that increases in static analysis warnings and code complexity are major factors driving long-term velocity slowdown.
Our study identifies quality assurance as a major bottleneck for early Cursor adopters and calls for it to be a first-class citizen in the design of agentic AI coding tools and AI-driven workflows.%
